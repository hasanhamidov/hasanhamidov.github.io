Securing Privileged Access in Critical Infrastructure

Attackers rarely need a zero-day when they can simply log in. Five practical principles for protecting the accounts they want most, in environments where availability and safety come first.

HH
Hasan Hamidov 26 Oct 2026 · 7 min read

Ask any incident responder how attackers moved from their first foothold to the systems that mattered, and the answer is usually the same: they used legitimate credentials. MITRE ATT&CK lists Valid Accounts (T1078) under four different tactics — initial access, persistence, privilege escalation and defence evasion — because a stolen administrator password is useful at almost every stage of an intrusion.

Privileged accounts are the keys to the network: domain administrators, local administrators, service accounts, database owners, firewall and hypervisor admins, and the engineering accounts that can change a control system. Whoever holds them can switch off logging, push software to every endpoint, or stop a process that people depend on.

Privileged access management (PAM) is the discipline of controlling those keys. This article looks at why it matters even more in critical infrastructure, and at five principles that make a PAM programme effective rather than just a box to tick.

Why critical infrastructure is different

Railways, energy grids, water utilities and telecom networks share a few traits that make privileged access both more important and harder to secure than in a typical office network:

  • Availability and safety come first. You cannot reboot a signalling server or rotate a password on a production controller whenever you like. Every security change has to fit around operations.
  • Long-lived and legacy systems. Equipment often runs for ten or twenty years. Older systems may only support shared local accounts, hard-coded credentials or protocols without modern authentication.
  • Many third parties. Vendors and integrators need remote access for maintenance, often to the most sensitive systems, and often with powerful accounts.
  • IT and OT meet. Corporate IT, engineering workstations and operational technology connect at a few points. An administrator account that crosses that boundary is a direct path from email phishing to physical processes.

Standards reflect this. IEC 62443, the main standard family for industrial automation security, puts identification, authentication and use control at the centre of its system requirements. NIST SP 800-53 describes the same idea as least privilege (AC-6): every user and process gets only the access it needs, and nothing more.

A PAM tool does not secure privileged access. A process does — the tool only makes the process enforceable.

Five principles that work

1. Know every privileged account

You cannot protect accounts you do not know exist. Discovery is the least exciting part of PAM and the most important. A complete inventory includes:

  • members of privileged directory groups (for example Domain Admins, Enterprise Admins and their nested groups);
  • local administrator accounts on servers and workstations;
  • service and application accounts, especially those with interactive logon rights or passwords that never expire;
  • network device, hypervisor, backup and database administrator accounts;
  • vendor and engineering accounts on operational systems;
  • emergency "break-glass" accounts.

Treat the inventory as a living asset. New systems arrive, projects create temporary admin accounts, and group memberships drift. Re-run discovery on a schedule and investigate anything that appears outside the approved process.

2. Grant as little as possible, for as short as possible

Least privilege has two dimensions: scope and time. Scope means an administrator for the ticketing system should not be a domain administrator. Time means that most people do not need standing privilege at all; they need it for the duration of a change.

Just-in-time access — privileges granted for a specific task and removed afterwards — reduces the window in which a stolen credential is useful. Combine it with tiering: separate the accounts that manage identity systems (tier 0), servers and applications (tier 1) and user workstations (tier 2), and never let a higher-tier account log on to a lower-tier machine, where its credentials could be harvested.

3. Vault and rotate credentials

If an administrator knows a password, that password can be phished, reused or written down. Storing privileged credentials in a vault and rotating them automatically changes that: people check out access, the vault records who did so, and the password changes afterwards.

Two quick wins stand out. First, give every machine a unique local administrator password; a single shared password turns one compromised host into all of them, which is exactly what techniques such as Pass the Hash (T1550.002) exploit. Windows LAPS solves this for Windows endpoints at no extra cost. Second, bring service accounts under management, or replace them with managed service accounts where the platform supports it.

In operational environments, test rotation carefully. Some applications store credentials in configuration files or scripts, and an unplanned rotation can stop a service. Map dependencies first and schedule the first rotation in a maintenance window.

4. Broker and record privileged sessions

Administrators should not connect directly from their everyday workstation to critical servers. Instead, privileged sessions go through a hardened jump host or session broker that injects the credential without revealing it, enforces approval where needed, and records the session.

This gives three benefits at once: credentials never touch a potentially compromised endpoint, there is a single choke point that firewalls can enforce, and there is an audit trail of exactly what was done. Session recordings are also valuable during investigations, because they show actions rather than just logon events.

5. Treat vendor access as high risk

Third-party remote access is one of the most common ways into industrial networks. Vendor access should be:

  • individual — named accounts, never a shared "vendor" login;
  • time-bound — enabled for an agreed window and disabled automatically afterwards;
  • approved — requested through a ticket and approved by the system owner;
  • brokered and recorded — through the same session controls as internal administrators.

PAM is also a detection sensor

From a SOC perspective, a well-run PAM programme does something extra: it defines what normal privileged activity looks like. Once all legitimate admin work flows through the vault and session broker, any privileged activity that does not is suspicious by definition.

That turns into concrete detections. On Windows, a few security events are particularly useful when correlated with PAM records:

Event IDMeaningWhy it matters
4624 (type 10)Successful remote interactive (RDP) logonPrivileged RDP logons that do not originate from the jump host bypass the broker.
4672Special privileges assigned to a new logonMarks administrator-level logons; useful for spotting privileged use outside approved sessions.
4728 / 4732 / 4756Member added to a global, local or universal security groupUnexpected additions to privileged groups are a classic persistence technique.
4625Failed logonBursts against privileged accounts can indicate password guessing or spraying.

Useful correlation ideas include a privileged logon with no matching vault checkout, a vaulted account used from an unexpected host, a break-glass account used at all, and changes to privileged group membership outside a change window. Each of these is cheap to implement and produces few false positives, because the baseline is so well defined.

Common mistakes

  • Vaulting without rotating. If the old password is still known, the vault adds little. Rotate immediately after onboarding an account.
  • Forgetting service accounts. They often have broad rights and passwords that have not changed in years.
  • Unmonitored break-glass accounts. Emergency accounts are necessary, but every use should raise an alert.
  • Leaving the PAM platform itself exposed. The vault holds the keys to everything. Treat it as a tier-0 asset, with its own hardening, monitoring and backups.
  • Making the secure path the slow path. If using the broker takes much longer than a direct connection, people will find workarounds. Usability is a security requirement.

A short checklist

  • We have a current inventory of all privileged accounts, including service, vendor and break-glass accounts.
  • Standing privilege is limited; most administrative access is granted just in time.
  • Privileged credentials are vaulted and rotated, and every machine has a unique local admin password.
  • Administrative sessions to critical systems go through a broker and are recorded.
  • Vendor access is individual, time-bound, approved and recorded.
  • The SOC alerts on privileged activity that bypasses the PAM workflow.

Privileged access management is often presented as a compliance project. In critical infrastructure it is much more than that: it is one of the few controls that directly limits how far an attacker can go after the first compromise. The open question I find most interesting is how to use PAM and session data as a high-quality signal for detection, and I plan to explore that further in future posts.
